Families do not all follow the same structure. Some families include more than two adults who share a home, contribute financially, care for children or regard each other as partners. Others include several significant and committed relationships across different households.

To the people involved, the family may feel settled, supportive and entirely ordinary. The law may see something more complicated.

Polyamory describes a relationship structure in which a person may have more than one committed partner at the same time, with the knowledge and agreement of everyone involved. 

In this article, we use the broader expression “multi-adult family” to describe a family or household in which more than two adults share significant relationships, financial arrangements, caregiving responsibilities or a common domestic life. Not every multi-adult family is polyamorous, and not every polyamorous relationship involves living together, sharing finances or raising children.

Australian family law does not presently recognise a multi-adult family as one collective marriage or one collective de facto relationship. Instead, it generally considers marriage and de facto status by reference to a relationship between two people. That does not mean a multi-adult family is less genuine. It does not mean that the adults involved have no legal rights. It also does not mean that the important relationships in a child’s life will simply be ignored. It means the legal position may need to be considered carefully, one relationship at a time.

Property ownership, financial contributions and parenting arrangements may not fit neatly into one family law matter. Different adults within the same family may have different rights, responsibilities and legal options.

For families whose lives do not fit traditional legal categories, clarity matters. Understanding where the law may provide protection, and where gaps may remain, can prevent significant uncertainty later.

## Can Australian law recognise more than one relationship?

A person cannot enter into more than one valid marriage in Australia at the same time. De facto relationships are different.

The *Family Law Act 1975* allows a de facto relationship to exist even if one person is legally married to someone else or is also in another de facto relationship. That means a person may potentially be in more than one legally recognised relationship at the same time.

However, the law does not recognise three or more adults together as one de facto relationship. Each potential de facto relationship must be assessed separately.

If three adults share a home and family life, the legal question is not simply whether the three people together form a family. The Court may need to consider whether a de facto relationship exists between Person A and Person B, between Person A and Person C, or between Person B and Person C. The answer may not be the same for every relationship.

This can feel artificial when the adults experience themselves as one family. However, it reflects the two-person structure of the current legislation. It also means that no one should assume their legal position simply from the language used within the family or the commitment everyone feels.

## How does the Court decide whether a defacto relationship exists?

Calling someone a partner does not automatically create a defacto relationship. Equally, having separate homes or bank accounts does not automatically prevent one from existing.

The central question is whether the two people had a relationship as a couple living together on a genuine domestic basis. The Court may consider how long the relationship lasted, whether and how the people shared a home, how they managed money and whether either person provided financial support to the other. It may also consider whether they acquired, owned or used property together, the degree of commitment to a shared life, the care and support of children and how the relationship was presented to family, friends and the wider community.

No single factor provides the answer. People do not necessarily need to live together every night. They do not need to combine all their money or own a home jointly. A person may also have more than one home. The Court looks at the reality of the relationship as a whole. This is particularly important in a multi-adult family because the relationships within the family may operate differently.

Two adults may share property, household expenses and daily responsibilities. Another relationship within the same family may involve deep affection and commitment but less financial or domestic integration. Those differences can affect whether each relationship is legally recognised.



## Personal commitment and legal status are not always the same

Most people do not begin a relationship by asking whether they satisfy the legal definition of a de facto couple. They build a life. They care for each other, share responsibilities, provide financial support and make plans. They may contribute to a home, work within a family business or help raise children without documenting precisely what those contributions mean. That can create uncertainty if one relationship later ends or the family structure changes.

One person may see financial support as part of building a shared future. Another may understand the same payments as gifts. One person may believe that contributing towards a mortgage gives them an interest in the home. Another may see those payments as a contribution towards the cost of living there. Neither person needs to have acted unfairly for a serious disagreement to arise. They may simply have been working from different assumptions.

The same applies to relationship status. Someone may describe another person as a partner because that language reflects a loving and committed relationship. That does not necessarily mean the legal de facto test is satisfied. The opposite assumption can be just as risky. A person may believe there cannot be a de facto relationship because another partner is involved, the adults maintain separate homes or their finances remain independent.

None of those facts decides the issue by itself. Early advice is not about forcing a family into a category. It is about understanding whether the law is likely to recognise the relationships and expectations that already exist.



## What does Jones & Mitchetti tell us?

The Federal Circuit and Family Court considered a long-term open relationship in *Jones & Michetti* \[2022] FedCFamC1F 771. The two people had been in a relationship for approximately 16 years. They spent regular time together, travelled together and provided care and support to one another. One person paid significant expenses for the other and included that person in a will. There was clearly affection and commitment.

However, they maintained separate homes, did not combine their day-to-day finances and did not acquire property together. One of them was also in another established de facto relationship. The Court accepted that a person may have more than one home and may be in more than one relationship. It also recognised that being in one de facto relationship does not legally prevent another de facto relationship from existing.

After considering the full circumstances, however, the Court found that the particular relationship before it had not developed into one where the two people were living together as a couple on a genuine domestic basis. The Court therefore declared that no de facto relationship existed. 

The case does not mean that relationships within multi-adult families cannot be legally recognised. It does show why assumptions can be risky. A relationship may be long, loving and significant without satisfying the legal test for a de facto relationship. Regular time together, financial generosity and the use of the word “partner” may all be relevant but none automatically decides the question.

The Court will look beyond how the relationship is described and consider how the two people arranged their domestic, financial and public lives.

## What happens when one relationship ends?

In a multi-adult family, not every relationship will necessarily begin or end at the same time. Two people may separate while other relationships continue. One adult may leave a shared household while the remaining adults stay in the home. A separation may also affect a business, investment or other property to which several people have contributed. This can make property issues particularly complex.

If an eligible marriage or de facto relationship ends, the people in that relationship may be able to seek a property settlement under the *Family Law Act*. However, the Court will not necessarily conduct one collective property settlement involving every adult in the family. There may instead be a property settlement between spouses, a separate claim between defacto partners or more than one potential claim concerning different relationships.

The position becomes more complicated where property is legally owned by another adult, several people have contributed to the same asset, or a company, trust or business is involved. Some issues may fall within the *Family Law Act*. Others may involve general property law, contract law or equitable principles. The correct approach will depend on the relationships, ownership structure and contributions involved.

An eligible de facto financial claim must also satisfy the relevant legal requirements. A two-year time limit generally applies after the de facto relationship ends, although the Court may grant permission to apply out of time in some circumstances. 

The difficulty is not only deciding what a fair outcome might be. It is first identifying which laws apply and who should be involved.

## When several people contribute to one home

Consider a home shared by three adults. One adult may provide the deposit. Another may make most of the mortgage repayments. The third may pay substantial household expenses, care for children or complete significant work on the property. Only one or two names may appear on the title.

If the household later changes, several questions may arise. Who legally owns the home? Were the payments gifts, loans, rent or contributions towards ownership? Was there an agreement about what each person would receive if the property were sold? Did someone rely on a promise that their interest would be recognised? The name on the title is important, but it may not always tell the whole story. The source of the funds, the purpose of the payments and any agreement between the adults may also matter.

At the same time, paying ordinary living expenses does not automatically give someone an ownership interest in another person’s property. Three people may contribute to the same household for years while holding three different understandings about what those contributions mean. That is why these issues are usually easier to address while everyone still agrees than after someone has left or the property is about to be sold.



## Clear financial arrangements can reduce uncertainty

Talking about legal ownership and money can feel uncomfortable when relationships are going well. It may seem overly formal to record whether a payment is a gift, a loan or a contribution towards property. We understand that. No one wants a conversation about building a family or buying a home to feel like a discussion about its end.

However, clarity is not the opposite of trust. Clear arrangements allow each person to make informed decisions. They reduce the risk that the same payment, promise or contribution means something entirely different to everyone involved. Useful records may include title and mortgage documents, bank transfers, loan agreements, renovation invoices and written communications recording what the adults agreed. Where a business, company, partnership or trust is involved, the governing documents may also need to reflect the arrangement.

The purpose is not to turn a family into a commercial transaction; it is to avoid important questions being answered for the first time during a dispute.

Was the deposit a gift or a loan? Was one person assisting with expenses or acquiring an interest in the home? Was unpaid work in a business intended to lead to ownership, or was it help provided without that expectation? There is no universal answer. What matters is that the people involved understand and record their own answer.



## Can a financial agreement help?

A financial agreement can help married couples and eligible de facto couples decide how property, debts, superannuation and financial support will be dealt with if their relationship ends. However, financial agreements under the *Family Law Act* operate by reference to a marriage or de facto relationship. They do not create legal recognition of one collective multi-adult relationship. A multi-adult family may therefore need several documents that work together.

Depending on the circumstances, this may involve separate financial agreements between particular people, together with a co-ownership agreement dealing with a shared home or investment. Loan arrangements may need to be documented separately. If a business, company or trust is involved, its governing documents should also be reviewed. An agreement between two people should not overlook another adult’s genuine interest in property. Equally, a co-ownership arrangement should be considered alongside any existing or proposed family law agreement.

Independent legal advice is mandatory before entering a financial agreement under the *Family Law Act*. A financial agreement may address all or only part of the financial relationship, but strict technical requirements apply.  Independent advice is not about creating sides within a family. It helps ensure that everyone understands the proposed arrangement and the rights they may be keeping or giving up.



## What about children?

Parenting law does not require a child’s family to look conventional; its focus is the child’s best interests. A child may have a close and secure relationship with an adult who is not a biological or legal parent. That person may have lived with the child, provided daily care, attended appointments, supported the child’s education or been a consistent source of stability. If the family arrangement changes, the child’s real relationships matter.

An adult does not automatically become a legal parent because that adult has played an important caregiving role. However, a person who is concerned with a child’s care, welfare or development may, in appropriate circumstances, apply for parenting orders. Parenting orders may deal with where a child lives, time with parents or other people, communication and responsibility for particular decisions. 

The issue is viewed from the child’s perspective; it is not about whether an adult feels entitled to continue the relationship. It is about what arrangements will best support the child’s safety, stability, important relationships and wellbeing.



## Agreements should reflect the child's lived experience

Where adults can reach agreement, parenting arrangements should reflect the family the child actually knows. A written arrangement may address where the child will live, the practical role of each important adult, communication, school and medical information, holidays, significant occasions and how future decisions will be made. The adults should also consider what may happen if one relationship ends or one person leaves the household.

Children should not be expected to carry adult uncertainty or decide which relationships remain important after a family change. A parenting plan may record an agreement, but it is not enforceable in the same way as a parenting order. Where greater certainty is required, advice should be obtained about consent orders and who should properly be involved.

The purpose is not to force a child’s family into traditional labels. It is to create arrangements that support the child’s real life and preserve important relationships where doing so is safe and in the child’s best interests.

## A brief note about future planning

Family law documents do not necessarily determine what happens if someone dies or can no longer make decisions. People in multi-adult families should obtain separate advice about wills, powers of attorney and superannuation nominations. A long-standing relationship may not automatically produce the outcome everyone expects.
