Not knowing what will happen can make mediation feel more daunting than it needs to be. You may be wondering whether you will have to sit in the same room as your former partner, explain the entire history of the relationship or make important decisions under pressure.

A good mediation should not feel like being placed in a room and told to sort everything out. It should be a considered process. You should understand what will happen, have an opportunity to raise safety or accessibility concerns and be given enough structure to participate meaningfully.

# Is mediation the same as Family Dispute Resolution?

Family Dispute Resolution, commonly called FDR, is a process designed to help people resolve some or all of the issues arising from separation. FDR is a form of mediation.

In family law, the terms “mediation” and “Family Dispute Resolution” are often used interchangeably. However, FDR conducted for parenting disputes has a particular legal framework. It is facilitated by an independent and registered Family Dispute Resolution Practitioner or FDRP.

An FDRP helps participants identify the issues, communicate and explore possible agreements. The practitioner does not take sides, impose an outcome or act as the lawyer for either person.

# The process begins before the mediation day

The first stage is a separate and confidential intake session with each participant. This is an important part of the process, not simply an administrative call.

The intake gives the FDRP an opportunity to understand the issues, assess whether mediation is suitable and discuss anything that may affect a person’s ability to participate safely and effectively.

It also gives you an opportunity to ask questions. You may wish to explain that direct communication is difficult, that you need additional processing time or that being in the same room as the other person would not feel safe. You may want written information, scheduled breaks or a support person involved. These matters are better raised before the mediation begins.

You do not need to know the formal name of the adjustment you require. You can simply explain what helps you communicate and what may make participation difficult.

At Hazel, each intake is conducted separately. The information helps us decide whether FDR is suitable and, if it is, how the process should be structured. 



# Preparing for the discussion

Preparation does not mean rehearsing an argument. It means understanding what needs to be resolved and arriving with the information required to have a useful conversation.

For a parenting mediation, this may involve thinking carefully about the children’s ordinary routines, schooling, health, activities, relationships and developmental needs. It can help to move beyond broad positions such as “I want equal time” and consider how a proposed arrangement would work on a school morning, during holidays or when a child is unwell.

For a property mediation, preparation generally requires a reliable understanding of the assets, liabilities, superannuation and financial resources. Valuations or accounting advice may be needed before meaningful negotiations can occur.

If important information is missing, mediation may still help the participants agree on how it will be obtained. However, it may not be sensible to make final financial decisions without understanding the financial position.

# Do we have to sit together?

Not necessarily. Some mediations take place with both participants in the same room. Others use shuttle mediation, where each person remains in a separate room and the FDRP moves between them. Online participation may also be appropriate in some circumstances.

The format should not be chosen simply because one method is standard. It should reflect the issues, safety considerations and ability of each person to participate.

Separate rooms may reduce direct conflict and allow each person time to consider proposals privately. A joint session may assist where participants can communicate productively and would benefit from hearing the same information together.

A person should not be required to participate face-to-face merely to prove a willingness to resolve the dispute.

At Hazel, sessions are ordinarily conducted in person, although online attendance may be arranged at the FDRP’s discretion. The appropriate structure is considered through the intake process. 

# What happens during the mediation?

The FDRP will explain the process, the practitioner’s role and the expectations for participation. The discussion will usually focus on the issues that need to be decided, rather than examining every grievance from the relationship.

Each person has an opportunity to explain what matters and why. The FDRP may help clarify the issues, test whether proposals are practica land encourage participants to consider alternatives.

The participants remain responsible for the outcome. The FDRP does not decide where the children will live, who should retain the home or what percentage each person should receive in a property settlement.

That is one of the important differences between mediation and Court. In mediation, the people affected retain control over whether an agreement is reached and what it contains.

# Will I be pressured to agree?

You should not be. Mediation involves negotiation and that can be challenging. You may hear proposals you do not accept or receive advice that requires careful thought. That is different from being pressured into an agreement.

You remain entitled to say no, ask for clarification, propose an alternative or request time to consider something. The goal is not agreement at any cost. It is a process in which informed and voluntary agreement is genuinely possible.

If you feel unable to think clearly, disagree safely or understand what is being proposed, tell the FDRP. A break, a change in formator further legal advice may be required.

# How long does mediation take?

At Hazel, a focused mediation concerning either parenting or property may be conducted over a half day of up to four hours. A full-day conference of up to seven hours may be recommended for complex matters or where parenting and property issues overlap.

More time does not necessarily mean better outcomes. Some matters resolve quickly because the issues are narrow and the participants are well prepared. Others require time to exchange proposals, obtain advice and work through practical detail.

It is also possible to resolve only some issues. Agreement about school holidays, communication or the process for valuing a property may still represent meaningful progress, even if everything is not finalised that day.

# What happens at the end?

If agreement is reached, the next step depends on what has been agreed and the formality required.

Parenting arrangements may be recorded in a parenting plan or made legally binding through consent orders. Property agreements may require consent orders or, in appropriate circumstances, a financial agreement.

An agreement reached at private FDR is not automatically binding or a Court order; it may need to be formalised through consent orders.

If parenting issues do not resolve, the FDRP may issue a section 60I certificate where permitted. The type of certificate will depend on what occurred, including whether both people attended and whether the matter was suitable to proceed. 

# Moving forward with clarity

FDR is not a test of who can argue most persuasively. It is an opportunity to step away from repeated conflict and have a more structured conversation about what happens next.
