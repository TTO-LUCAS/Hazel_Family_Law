Being told that your behaviour is frightening, controlling or harmful can be confronting. You may disagree with what has been alleged, believe particular events have been taken out of context or worry that seeking support will be treated as an admission. You may also recognise that conflict has escalated and that some of your responses are affecting your former partner, your children or your own wellbeing.

Seeking legal advice or behavioural support does not require you to accept every allegation. An allegation is not the same as a finding. At the same time, disputing an allegation does not remove the need to comply with intervention orders, respect boundaries or consider whether some behaviour needs to change.

There should be space for both truths: people experiencing family violence deserve to be heard and protected and people concerned about their own behaviour need a genuine pathway towards accountability and change.



## The research behind this article

Through Elisa’s role as a member of the Law Institute of Victoria Family Violence Working Group, she recently attended a presentation by the researchers behind the Male Perpetrator Interventions for Domestic and Family Violence in the Indo-Pacific: A Scoping Review.

The presentation prompted an important practical question: how do we prioritise safety and accountability without treating a person seeking help as incapable of change?

The review mapped 42 interventions across 14 Indo-Pacific countries. Almost half were based in Australia, many extended beyond conventional behaviour-change programs and almost half involved a whole-of-community response. The researchers also identified gaps in interventions responsive to culture, indigeneity, disability, gender and sexual diversity. 

The research does not suggest there is one program that will work for everyone. It highlights the need to consider the person, the behaviour and the circumstances in which that behaviour occurs.



## When conflict may have become something more

Separation can involve anger, grief and difficult communication. Not every disagreement, impatient message or poorly handled conversation amounts to family violence. The concern becomes more serious where behaviour is repeated, creates fear, restricts another person’s choices or is used to force a particular outcome. That can include threats, intimidation, persistent monitoring, property damage, financial restriction or repeated hostile communication. It may also involve using children, parenting arrangements, money or legal processes to pressure another person.

Under the *Family Law Act 1975* (Cth), family violence includes violent, threatening or other behaviour that coerces or controls a family member or causes that person to be fearful. Family violence can occur before, during or after separation and can affect a person’s ability to make choices and participate in family law proceedings. 

Context matters; so does the pattern and effect of the conduct.

It may help to ask whether you are trying to resolve an issue or compel agreement. Can the other person disagree without fearing what will happen next? Are you continuing to contact them after being asked to stop? Are children being exposed to conflict, carrying messages or being asked for information about another household?

These questions are not about attaching an identity to you. They help identify whether conflict has moved beyond an ordinary disagreement and whether support is needed.



## Accountability can leave room for change

A person should not be reduced to an allegation or defined entirely by conduct that can change. Accountability does, however, require a willingness to consider what occurred, how it affected others and what needs to happen differently. That may mean complying with an intervention order while allegations remain disputed, changing how communication occurs or respecting boundaries that were not previously observed.

It can be difficult to engage constructively when a person feels labelled, ashamed or afraid of losing important relationships. A constructive response creates room for something more useful: understanding the concerns, taking responsibility where appropriate and demonstrating change through future conduct.

The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia describes family violence as preventable and recognises that lasting change depends on work both within and beyond the justice system. Its prevention guidance emphasises leadership, partnership and shared responsibility rather than blame alone. 



## Why the right support may involve more than managing anger

Anger may be part of the picture but harmful behaviour is not always caused by a momentary loss of temper. Behaviour may also be connected with difficulty accepting separation, managing rejection or uncertainty or respecting another person’s independent decisions. Appropriate support may need to consider communication, emotional regulation, parenting, alcohol or drug use, mental health, social isolation and patterns of control. These circumstances do not excuse harmful conduct. Understanding them can help identify the support most likely to reduce risk and create lasting change.

Attending a program can be an important beginning but attendance alone does not establish that risk has ended. Meaningful change is usually demonstrated over time. It may be reflected in compliance with orders, respectful communication, acceptance of boundaries and an end to threatening, monitoring or retaliatory conduct.

For children, change also means being kept away from adult disputes. Children should not carry messages, report on another household or be made responsible for managing an adult’s reactions.



## Legal advice and behavioural support have different roles

A family lawyer can explain intervention orders, parenting proceedings, communication restrictions and the consequences of breaching an order. A lawyer cannot provide behaviour change treatment.

Similarly, a behaviour change practitioner can support reflection and safer choices but cannot advise about Court orders or legal strategy. Both forms of assistance may be important.
